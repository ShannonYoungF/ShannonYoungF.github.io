Human beings have long translated the world into forms that can be preserved, shared, and used for reasoning.

Writing records experience, mathematics describes relationships, images preserve appearances, and code expresses rules that can be executed. In digital environments, these records are further encoded as discrete units of information, allowing people across different times and places to build knowledge together.

Today, AI, particularly large models, learns concepts, relationships, and patterns from vast collections of these records. Material once scattered across books, papers, images, and programs can be connected and drawn upon in new ways.

From this perspective, AI can be understood as a compressed reorganization, within machines, of humanity's extensive records of the world.

This view has limits. What people have recorded is only part of the world, and what AI encounters and learns is only part of those records. They contain verified knowledge alongside misconceptions, biases, and fiction. The representations AI develops carry the achievements of human understanding, together with its limitations.

Even so, this reorganization matters. It makes connections between bodies of knowledge easier to discover and makes explorations that once required crossing professional boundaries more accessible.

A finite set of symbols can also refer to structures far richer than the symbols themselves. A short formula can define a curve containing infinitely many points. Under suitable conditions, a set of differential equations can determine how a continuous system evolves. Rules allow us to describe relationships between states without listing every state individually.

Discrete representations can therefore help us understand continuous processes. The real difficulties concern whether finite observations are sufficient to identify a governing relationship, and how widely that relationship holds. AI can contribute to this work, but the connections, models, and hypotheses it proposes must also be tested through experiments and feedback from the world.

As large bodies of knowledge become easier to draw upon, another question deserves attention: what can people create with these capabilities?

In a growing range of settings, the costs of retrieving information, developing initial explanations, generating proposals, and building prototypes are falling. Identifying worthwhile problems, understanding specific needs, judging the quality of proposals, and obtaining real feedback may consequently become more important.

The long-term value of AI will emerge through its combination with people at these points.

Scientific research is one important setting. Researchers formulate questions, while AI helps connect findings across fields, identify possible explanations, design experiments, and revise models in light of the results. Developing a new material, for example, can proceed through repeated cycles of performance targets, candidate structures, preparation conditions, and test results. Combining human experience and a sense of which questions matter with machines' capacity for large-scale search and reasoning may shorten exploration cycles and reveal paths that had not previously been considered. New knowledge ultimately requires evidence, and AI can help us reach the point of obtaining that evidence more effectively.

Changes in education will influence how many people can participate in this kind of creation. People differ in their prior knowledge, ways of understanding, and pace of learning. A student's repeated mistakes may reflect a conceptual misunderstanding that has not yet been identified. Through sustained interaction, AI can help identify these differences and adapt explanations and exercises so that students gradually develop the ability to solve unfamiliar problems. Teachers provide direction, encouragement, and understanding grounded in shared experience; AI supports more detailed individual guidance. This collaboration can extend to adults, giving more people a chance to move beyond their original fields, develop interests into skills, and turn experience into research questions.

The creative capacity of individuals and small teams may expand as well. Many people have spent years in an industry and understand its recurring problems, yet lack the technical resources to build solutions. AI can help them clarify requirements, write programs, build prototypes, test functions, and prepare documentation. Creators can also use these tools to develop local historical sources, personal experiences, or new narrative ideas into films, interactive works, or public exhibitions. People who understand a problem thereby gain a more direct opportunity to take part in solving it.

In healthcare and care work, much of the value lies in developing an understanding over time. A person's health information is scattered across medical records, examinations, medication histories, and everyday life. A single consultation can rarely capture the full course of change. AI can help connect this information, support doctors in identifying trends worth investigating and comparing options, and help patients understand and follow care plans. Doctors, patients, and families still need to discuss goals and trade-offs together. Better organization of information and continued monitoring may give those discussions a stronger basis.

When AI is combined with sensors, robots, and engineering systems, knowledge can also guide physical action. An agricultural system, for instance, can use information about soil, weather, and crop conditions to help plan irrigation. Equipment carries out the plan, and measured results inform the next round of operations. Similar cycles can take place in manufacturing, energy, construction, and laboratories. Models in these settings must contend with real constraints imposed by materials, equipment, environments, and time, making their value more directly testable through results.

Collaboration in public affairs brings another set of requirements. A city dealing with water supply, transport, or extreme heat needs to understand many interacting factors at once. AI can help organize information, explore possible plans, and show how benefits and costs may be distributed across different groups. The public and decision-makers can then discuss goals, priorities, and consequences. Better computation can inform those discussions, while conflicts between goals still require social negotiation.

The division of work in these settings will change. AI can help people discover problems and evaluate proposals, while people also need to engage with technical details and understand how models work. Effective collaboration depends on connecting knowledge, tools, human experience, and feedback from the world.

Creative value must also be judged through these connections. A novel proposal may solve no practical problem, while an unobtrusive improvement may reduce repetitive work for many people or help those who have previously been overlooked. Maintainable software, a useful assistive tool, and a more effective way of teaching can all have creative value.

As the ability to generate grows, careful selection and validation become more important. We need to ask whom a proposal serves, what it improves, what it costs, and how its results can be demonstrated. These questions gradually turn abstract possibilities into outcomes that can be understood and evaluated.

Scientific discovery, education, and the expansion of individual creative capacity deserve particular long-term investment. Research increases the knowledge available to us. Education increases the number of people able to understand and use it. Tools for creation help more people turn knowledge into works, products, and action. These activities have the potential to reinforce one another over time.

People have expressed their understanding of the world in writing, formulas, images, and code. We are now beginning to use AI to reorganize these records and subject new combinations to the test of reality.

A connection may become a hypothesis; a hypothesis may lead to an experiment; an experiment may change a tool; and a tool that proves useful may change people's lives. The value of people and AI creating together will take shape through these concrete processes.
