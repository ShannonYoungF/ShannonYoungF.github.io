Is AI a Discrete Reorganization of the Universe?

AI reconnects the words, images, equations, and code we have created to understand the world. Is it reorganizing the universe, or our descriptions of it?

2026.11.04 4 min read Last updated 2026.11.04
Artificial IntelligenceRepresentationKnowledgeReflection

A question occurred to me recently: if we keep describing the universe through language, mathematics, images, and code, and AI can learn from and recombine those descriptions, could we call AI a discrete reorganization of the universe at some level?

The thought is tempting. It places AI beyond the role of a tool that answers questions: it becomes a system assembled from accumulated human representations, capable of generating new ones. But for the thought to hold, I think we first need to ask what exactly is being reorganized.

We Have Never Handed the Universe Directly to a Model

What we first encounter is not text. We encounter perception, measurement, and feedback from action. We name phenomena, design instruments, record data, choose coordinate systems, write relationships as equations, and turn equations into executable programs.

The same process of heat diffusion can appear as a temperature image, a set of sensor readings, a partial differential equation, a numerical solver, or an article about the heat kernel. These representations point toward related phenomena, but none is the phenomenon itself. Each preserves some relationships and omits some details.

Today's generative AI encounters these recorded traces, and only a portion of them. Much experience was never recorded; much of what was recorded is not publicly accessible; and even material present in training data is not necessarily retained in full. “AI contains every discrete representation humanity has built to describe the universe” is therefore a useful limiting thought experiment, not a factual description of existing models.

A more defensible formulation may be: AI learns from a finite collection of human representations and tries to recover relationships that transfer between them.

The Interesting Part Is Reconnection

If the task were merely to store existing texts, libraries would have accomplished it long ago. What surprises me about AI is its ability to build bridges between descriptions that were previously separated.

When trying to understand kernel functions, for example, I can begin with discrete weights in matrix multiplication, move to a continuous kernel in an integral operator, and then connect that picture to function inner products, spectral decomposition, and neural operators. These concepts are scattered across textbooks and papers. A model can place them in one reasoning context and suggest a possible path toward understanding.

That does not mean it has uncovered a hidden truth of the universe. It may offer a false analogy or merge theorems whose assumptions differ. Yet this ability to connect representations changes how we approach a problem. Clues that once required a long search across disciplines can now be juxtaposed, compared, and tested quickly.

This is the meaning of “reorganization” I most want to keep: not taking the world apart and assembling a second world, but rearranging the routes by which humans describe it, so concepts that were far apart can meet.

What Does “Discrete” Mean Here?

Text consists of symbols. Images and sounds can be sampled into pixels and numbers. Equations and programs follow explicit notation rules. Such representations let information be copied, transmitted, computed, and combined. Modern models also commonly split inputs into tokens; computers ultimately store data at finite precision.

Still, a model is not simply an enormous discrete dictionary. Its learned weights and intermediate representations take part in computation as numerical vectors. Whether any particular discrete representation could adequately describe the world is a separate question that needs an argument of its own.

So when I call AI a “discrete reorganization,” I am referring to the recording and computational methods it depends on. I am not claiming that digitization has exhausted the universe. There remains a gap between the form of information and the reality it points toward.

Finding Our Way Back to the World

I increasingly think that the value of a new connection depends less on how elegant it sounds than on whether it can withstand questions from the world.

If AI connects the heat kernel, the diffusion equation, and Fourier analysis, we still need to check the conditions of the derivation, run the code, compare experimental data, and see whether predictions hold. A model offers a path to investigate. Measurement, practice, and discussion with others help determine whether that path has actually improved our understanding.

This leads me to a harder question: how far can a system go toward understanding the world if it only finds relationships among existing descriptions? As models gain access to sensors, tools, and experimental workflows, do they receive richer descriptions, or a fundamentally different kind of contact? I do not think that boundary is settled.

A Provisional Answer

I would keep the bold opening metaphor for now, with one qualification: AI may be a computable reorganization of humanity's picture of the universe, rather than a copy of the universe itself.

That qualification does not diminish the idea. Precisely because our picture is partial and revisable, reconnecting its parts matters. A new connection may be an elegant illusion, or it may become a productive hypothesis. We can tell the difference only by returning to the world.

What I want to keep asking is this: when AI helps us pose a question we had not thought to ask, where does the new understanding happen? Inside the model, in the collaboration between person and model, or in the reality that can be changed and tested through an experiment?
